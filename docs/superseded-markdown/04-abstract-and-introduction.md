## Abstract

Ethiopia has, in twenty-four months, acquired most of the formal apparatus of an AI-ready state: a National Artificial Intelligence Policy adopted by the Council of Ministers in June 2024, a Personal Data Protection Proclamation in force since July 2024, a Digital Ethiopia 2030 strategy adopted in December 2025, a dedicated Artificial Intelligence Institute whose budget rose 42 percent to ETB 1.13 billion, and a specialist AI university approved in March 2026. Over the same period, Microsoft's diffusion measurements placed Ethiopian generative-AI use below eight percent of the working-age population — near the bottom of 147 economies — and the World Bank's *World Development Report 2026* classified the country as underperforming on both the capacity to produce AI and the capacity to adopt it. Institutional ambition and observed diffusion are moving in opposite directions, and the size of the gap between them has never been measured with Ethiopian primary data.

This study measures it. We report the first multi-sector primary-data survey of artificial intelligence adoption in Ethiopia, combining an organisational survey of [n] institutions drawn from a published sampling frame spanning federal and regional government, banking, telecommunications, health, education, agriculture, research and the private sector; a practitioner survey of [n] technology professionals, researchers and educators; a citizen pulse survey of [n] respondents fielded through Telegram, interactive voice response and an enumerator-administered rural booster sample; and [n] key-informant interviews. Adoption is not self-rated. It is scored against a six-level maturity ladder using gated behavioural evidence — a named system, a go-live date, identifiable users, a budget line, production monitoring — so that intent, procurement and pilot activity cannot be recorded as deployment. We report both the self-rating and the evidence-computed score, and we treat the gap between them as a finding in its own right.

Our central results are [survey: headline adoption rate; the share of organisations with any system in production; the mean self-versus-computed maturity gap; the pilot-to-production conversion rate]. We find that variation in adoption is predicted [survey: more/less] by access to foreign currency, a foreign technology partner and a single internal champion than by sector — a result with direct implications for how AI policy should be targeted in a foreign-exchange-constrained economy. We document the first national inventory of AI computing capacity, [survey: n] accelerators across [survey: n] institutions with a mean utilisation of [survey: %], and we quantify the Ethiopian-language performance gap simultaneously at citizen, practitioner and organisational level — the point at which a country of some eighty-five languages either includes or excludes most of its population from every downstream AI service.

We then set out a prescriptive agenda. Against Ethiopia's actual factor endowments — abundant young labour, a grid that is roughly ninety-seven percent renewable, scarce capital, scarce data, scarce compute and exceptional linguistic diversity — we argue that the returns lie in adopting and adapting existing systems rather than in frontier production, in use cases where the data already exists as a by-product of a digital process, and in applications that substitute for scarce professionals rather than for abundant labour. We specify the foundational data assets each priority use case requires, together with their current custodians and condition; we cost three infrastructure scenarios on the principle that the cheapest compute is the compute one does not buy; and we propose a National Language and Data Commons as the country's most defensible differentiated investment.

Finally, we examine the institutional configuration itself. Under the current policy a single body drafts the national AI policy, coordinates its implementation, licenses private AI developers, certifies AI products both domestic and imported, evaluates the policy's success, and competes as a research and development producer with the universities and firms it regulates. We document the real contributions of that arrangement and the risks of concentrating six functions in one institution, and we recommend unbundling them into a policy secretariat, an independent regulator, an arm's-length funder operating by peer review, and a performer competing on equal terms. We close with a costed, owned and dated implementation roadmap of the kind Ethiopia's strategy currently lacks and Kenya's now has, and with an indicator set for a National AI Observatory for which this survey is the baseline.

The instrument, anonymised microdata, codebook and analysis code are released openly so that the baseline can be re-run, the findings contested, and the trend claimed by anyone.

**Keywords:** artificial intelligence adoption · Ethiopia · AI policy · digital divide · low-resource languages · AI governance · technology diffusion · Global South

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 A widening gap between apparatus and adoption

Between June 2024 and March 2026 Ethiopia assembled, in rapid succession, nearly every formal instrument that international readiness frameworks ask a developing country to produce. The Council of Ministers adopted a National Artificial Intelligence Policy whose stated vision is for Ethiopia to become "the centre of AI development excellence in Africa by 2035". A Personal Data Protection Proclamation entered into force. A Digital Ethiopia 2030 strategy replaced its predecessor and proposed a National Data Authority. The Ethiopian Artificial Intelligence Institute's appropriation rose by 42 percent to ETB 1.13 billion in a fiscal year of considerable pressure. A specialist artificial intelligence university — reportedly only the world's second — was approved, with a target of a thousand graduates a year.

On the readiness indices, this counted. Ethiopia became only the second low-income country, after Rwanda, to publish a national AI strategy.

Over precisely the same period, the measurements that track what people and organisations actually *do* pointed the other way. Microsoft's *Global AI Diffusion Report* for the first quarter of 2026 placed generative-AI use in Ethiopia below eight percent of the working-age population, among the lowest of the 147 economies it covers, and well beneath Kenya's 8.7 percent, Nigeria's 10.1 percent and South Africa's 23.1 percent. The World Bank's *World Development Report 2026* classified Ethiopia as a low-income country underperforming on both dimensions of AI readiness — lacking not only the market size and infrastructure to develop AI, but the per-capita capacity to adopt and use it across public services, businesses and daily life. Internet penetration stood at roughly 21.7 percent. Research and development spending was around 0.27 percent of GDP against an African Union target of one percent. Approximately six percent of Ethiopian technology startups were AI-focused, and the entire ecosystem had attracted some five million dollars in tracked funding — less than a single mid-sized funding round in Nairobi or Lagos.

Two conclusions are available. The optimistic one is that policy leads and diffusion follows, and that the gap is simply a lag. The pessimistic one is that institutional apparatus and economic diffusion are only loosely coupled, and that a country can accumulate strategies, institutes and universities while the organisations that would actually use AI remain where they were.

Distinguishing between these is an empirical question. It has not been answered, because **no one has measured AI adoption in Ethiopia using Ethiopian primary data.**

### 1.2 What this study does

This paper reports the first multi-sector primary-data survey of artificial intelligence adoption in Ethiopia, alongside a prescriptive agenda for AI value creation grounded in that evidence. It answers five questions:

1. **State.** What is the actual level, depth and distribution of AI adoption across Ethiopian sectors, organisation types and regions — as distinct from announced intent?
2. **Determinants.** Which technological, organisational and environmental factors explain the variation?
3. **Value.** Where does AI create the most defensible value given Ethiopia's factor endowments?
4. **Foundations.** What foundational data and cost-effective infrastructure does that require, and what does it cost?
5. **Governance.** Is the present institutional configuration fit for purpose?

### 1.3 How this study differs from existing work

Six bodies of work already describe AI in Ethiopia. Each is useful; none can answer the questions above, and the reasons are structural rather than a matter of quality.

**National policy documents** — the National AI Policy, Digital Ethiopia 2030 — establish intent and mandate. They contain no measurement of any kind. The AI Policy in particular sets a 2035 vision with no quantified target, no budget envelope and no dated milestone, and it was authored under the leadership of the institution charged with implementing it. A policy cannot serve as evidence of its own effect.

**Global readiness indices** — Oxford Insights' Government AI Readiness Index, ITU indicators, Microsoft's diffusion series — provide cross-country comparability and are the source of most numbers currently quoted about Ethiopian AI. They are built from secondary macro proxies and platform telemetry. They contain, so far as we can determine, **no Ethiopian survey respondents at all**. They cannot say which sectors have deployed what, why pilots fail, or whether a policy changed anything, because they never ask anyone in Ethiopia.

**Multilateral landscape studies** — most importantly UNESCO's 2026 *Sociotechnical Landscape of AI in Ethiopia* and the Readiness Assessment Methodology behind it — are the closest existing work and a genuine advance. They map institutions, law and infrastructure with care, and identify real gaps, including the absence of binding rules on AI procurement, algorithmic transparency and liability. But they are desk-based and institutional in lens: they describe what the state has built, not what organisations across the economy are running. They do not produce organisation-level microdata, and they do not reach beyond the institutional elite.

**Single-sector academic studies** — the evaluation of an AI-generated assessment instrument in Ethiopian mathematics teacher professional development, the agricultural pilots in poultry, dairy and enset disease surveillance — provide depth that no survey can match. By construction they generalise to nothing beyond their sector, and no framework yet places them side by side.

**Continental analyses** — the AU Continental AI Strategy, comparative work on the African AI divide — set the regional frame within which Ethiopia must be understood. Ethiopia appears in them as one row in a table, when it appears at all.

**Ethiopian NLP research** — EthioNLP, EthioLLM, EthioMT, the Ethiopian Language Survey — is the strongest and most internationally visible body of Ethiopian AI work, and it constitutes the real inventory of what exists for Ethiopian languages. It is technical rather than policy-facing, and it has never been linked to measured adoption outcomes.

This study makes five contributions that none of the above provides.

**First, it measures adoption with primary data from a published sampling frame.** Organisations are drawn from named registries — the National Bank's licensee list, the Ministry of Health facility registry, education and regional directories — with a census of the small strata and stratified random sampling within the large ones. Response rates are reported by stratum and non-response bias is tested. This is the difference between an estimate and an anecdote, and it is what makes the 2028 repeat comparable.

**Second, it measures diffusion rather than intent, and is designed to resist over-claiming.** In a context where AI carries strong political favour, self-reported adoption will be overstated. Four mechanisms are built into the instrument: mandatory evidence prompts that require any claimed deployment to be named, dated and attributed to identifiable users; a behavioural checklist from which the maturity level is *computed* under gated rules rather than self-assigned, so that training and planning activity cannot be scored as deployment; a definitional gate distinguishing AI from ordinary automation, dashboards and rules-based workflows; and a planted item naming a plausible but non-existent capability, whose selection rate is reported as a data-quality statistic. **The gap between self-rated and evidence-computed maturity is not a nuisance to be corrected away. It is one of the study's primary findings**, and to our knowledge it has not been reported for any African country.

**Third, it treats language as a measured variable rather than an assumption.** Ethiopia has roughly eighty-five languages. Every AI service is, in practice, gated by whether it works in the language the user speaks. We field the instruments in Amharic alongside English, with Afaan Oromo, Tigrinya and Somali versions following a full back-translation and cognitive-interviewing protocol, and we ask parallel questions at all three levels — whether a citizen found the tool worked in their language, whether a practitioner is limited by having to work in English, and whether the absence of Ethiopian-language support has ever stopped an organisation deploying a system. This converts a claim that everyone in the field repeats into a quantity with a confidence interval, and it connects the existing NLP resource inventory to observed adoption outcomes for the first time.

**Fourth, it reaches respondents that web surveys do not.** A web-only instrument in Ethiopia yields an Addis-based, English-reading, university-educated sample and a badly wrong national picture. We therefore field the citizen instrument through Telegram — the country's dominant social and information platform, workable at low bandwidth and native in Ethiopic script — supplemented by interactive voice response for respondents without smartphones and an enumerator-administered probability sample in rural and low-connectivity woredas, used both as a standalone estimate and to calibrate the online sample. We report weighted and unweighted estimates side by side and state plainly what a Telegram sample can and cannot support.

**Fifth, it is open, reproducible and contestable.** The instrument, codebook, anonymised microdata and analysis code are released under CC BY 4.0, with statistical disclosure control on the citizen file and a restricted tier for the organisational file. Hypotheses and the analysis plan are pre-registered before the main field wave. This matters more than usual here, because the paper assesses an institution that also funds much of the sector: independence has to be demonstrable rather than asserted, and critics must be able to check the work with the same data.

### 1.4 A note on the governance analysis

Section 4.2 examines the Ethiopian Artificial Intelligence Institute in detail, because the policy assigns it an unusually wide set of functions: drafting the policy, coordinating implementation, licensing private developers, certifying AI products both domestic and imported, operating the monitoring framework that judges the policy, and performing research and development in competition with the universities and firms it regulates. We document the Institute's real contributions — its convening power, its work on national-language technology, the agricultural pilots, and the simple fact that AI reached the Council of Ministers agenda at all — and we analyse the risks that follow from concentrating those functions.

That analysis is offered as institutional design, not as criticism of individuals, and it is deliberately structured to be falsifiable: the survey asks the sector directly whether it wants the Institute as its regulator, whether licensing would help or hinder, and whether the Institute has been practically useful, using balanced item wording with both positively and critically framed statements and an explicit "don't know" option. Key-informant interviews on governance are confidential and non-attributed by default, and named institutions are offered a right of reply before publication.

### 1.5 Structure

Section 2 sets out the conceptual framework and the adoption maturity ladder. Section 3 places Ethiopia in global, Global South, African and East African context, and identifies which peer approaches are transferable. Section 4 presents the empirical core: the policy environment, the institutional landscape, adoption across industries, the adopter typology, research capacity, the private sector, infrastructure, skills, funding flows and public attitudes. Section 5 sets out where AI should create value, use case by use case. Section 6 specifies the foundational data required and the standardisation agenda, including the Ethiopic encoding and Ethiopian-calendar interoperability problems that quietly defeat record linkage across national systems. Section 7 costs three infrastructure scenarios. Section 8 assesses challenges and risks, including those that AI strategies for Ethiopia habitually omit — foreign-exchange rationing, network shutdowns and implementation in a conflict-affected polity. Section 9 gives recommendations sequenced as *now*, *next* and *later*, each with an owner, a cost band and an indicator. Section 10 concludes.

### 1.6 A statement of position

We do not think Ethiopia will be the centre of AI excellence in Africa by 2035, and we think pursuing that framing misallocates scarce resources. We do think there is a proposition available to Ethiopia that is achievable, differentiated and more valuable: to become the country that made AI work under low bandwidth, low income and radical linguistic plurality, and that built the language and data commons the rest of the continent comes to depend on. That is a smaller claim than the policy makes. It is also one Ethiopia could actually win, and it happens to sit exactly where the country's demonstrated research strength already lies.
